Singular Hessians & Phase Fields

Three papers on reconstructing surfaces from unoriented point clouds, explained from the ground up — and a three-day plan to reimplement all three and come back with pictures.

He Ying (Dr) — 31 Aug 2026

“Have you tried to re-implement any of these algorithms? If so, show me some reconstruction results based on your own implementations.”

Read that request carefully. He is not asking for a literature review, and he is not asking for state-of-the-art numbers. He is asking whether you can turn equations into working code and produce an image of a surface. The deliverable is reconstructions you made, plus honest words about what worked and what did not. Three rough results with a clear account of their limitations beats one polished result you cannot explain.

Part 0Ground floor: what all three papers assume you already know

Skip nothing here. Every disagreement between the three papers lives in this section.

A surface as the zero set of a function

A mesh stores a surface explicitly: a list of vertices and triangles. An implicit representation stores it as the solution set of an equation. Pick a function f : ℝ³ → ℝ. The surface is every point where it equals zero:

𝒮 = { x ∈ ℝ³ : f(x) = 0 }level set

A sphere of radius r is f(x) = ‖x‖ − r. The appeal: f is continuous, so the surface has infinite resolution; it is differentiable, so you get normals and curvature for free; and a neural network is a natural way to store an arbitrary f. That last point is the whole field — neural implicit representation means “the function f is an MLP, and we fit its weights to one point cloud.”

Two flavours of f

  • SDF — signed distance function. |f(x)| is the distance to the surface, and the sign says inside (negative) or outside (positive). Requires a coherent notion of “inside”.
  • UDF — unsigned distance function. Just the distance, always ≥ 0. No inside needed.

Paper 1 (NSH) learns an SDF. Paper 2 (DEUDF) learns a UDF. Paper 3 (MPF) argues you should learn both, separately, and combine them. That is the entire arc.

Getting a mesh back out: marching cubes

To look at your result you need triangles. Evaluate f on a regular grid — say 192³ — and for every little cube, check whether f changes sign across its edges. Where it does, the surface passes through; a lookup table says which triangles to emit. That is marching cubes.

Notice the dependency: marching cubes needs a sign change. A UDF touches zero and bounces back up without ever going negative, so marching cubes at level 0 finds nothing at all. This single fact is responsible for a large fraction of paper 2 and paper 3.

The Eikonal equation

If f really is a distance function, then moving one unit in the direction of steepest ascent increases the distance by exactly one unit. In symbols:

‖∇f(x)‖₂ = 1eikonal

This is the workhorse regulariser of the whole field. You cannot supervise f away from the point cloud — you have no ground-truth distances — but you can insist that f behaves like a distance function everywhere. Add “f(p) = 0 at the input points” and you have IGR/SIREN-style fitting. All three papers start here and then argue about what else is needed.

Why “unoriented” is the hard word in the title

A point cloud is just positions. If each point also carried a correctly-oriented normal — a unit vector pointing consistently outward — reconstruction would be comparatively easy; that is what Poisson reconstruction consumes. Real scanners do not give you that. You can estimate a normal direction at each point by local PCA (fit a plane to the 16 nearest neighbours; the smallest-eigenvalue eigenvector is the normal), but its sign is arbitrary, and making the signs globally consistent is itself a hard combinatorial problem.

So: any loss you write must be invariant to flipping a normal. Watch for this. DEUDF gets it by sampling symmetric point pairs; MPF gets it by putting an absolute value around an inner product. If you ever find yourself using ground-truth oriented normals as supervision, you have quietly changed the problem into an easier one.

SIREN, and why the activation function matters

All three papers use the same backbone: an MLP with sin(ω₀ · Wx + b) activations instead of ReLU. Two reasons, both load-bearing:

  • ReLU has zero second derivative almost everywhere. Paper 1 is built entirely on the Hessian of f. With a ReLU network that Hessian is identically zero — the method could not exist.
  • ReLU networks are spectrally biased toward low frequencies, so they smooth away exactly the fine geometric detail all three papers are chasing.

ω₀ controls the frequency band: 30 is standard, DEUDF uses 60 on clean clouds and drops to 30 on noisy ones (high frequency fits noise). SIREN also needs its own weight initialisation — uniform in ±√(6/n)/ω₀ — without which it simply does not train.

Paper 1 · TOG 2023NSH — regularise the SDF with a singular Hessian

Neural-Singular-Hessian: Implicit Neural Representation of Unoriented Point Clouds by Enforcing Singular Hessian. Wang, Zhang, Xu, Zhang, Wang, Chen, Xin, Wang, Tu.

The problem it attacks

“f = 0 on the points” plus “‖∇f‖ = 1” does not pin down a unique f. Infinitely many wiggly functions satisfy both, and what you get in practice is ghost geometry: blobs and sheets floating in empty space, extra bumps where the point cloud was sparse.

The standard fix was to add a smoothness penalty — Laplacian energy (DiGS) or Hessian energy ‖H‖². It works, and it also destroys detail, because smoothness is indifferent to whether the wiggle it is flattening is noise or a real crease. Look at NSH's Fig. 2: DiGS produces a clean but soft dragon. NSH wants the creases back.

The idea

Rather than penalise variation, enforce a property that the true SDF provably has. Here is the geometry. Take a point x close to the surface and let x′ be its closest surface point. The Hessian of the true SDF at x has three eigenvectors:

  • two align with the principal curvature directions of the surface at x′, with eigenvalues related to the curvatures;
  • one aligns with the surface normal at x′ — and its eigenvalue is exactly zero.

Why zero? Along the normal direction the SDF changes perfectly linearly (walk one unit away, distance goes up by one), and the second derivative of a linear function is zero. A symmetric matrix with a zero eigenvalue is singular, so its determinant vanishes:

det( HSDF(x) ) = 0   for all x near the surfacethe whole paper

Why a determinant and not a norm

This is the point to have ready when he asks. Hessian energy is ‖H‖²; driving it to zero forces all nine entries to zero, which makes f an affine function — perfectly smooth and geometrically useless. Driving det(H) to zero removes one degree of freedom: it says the three eigenvalues cannot all be nonzero, and says nothing about how large the other two may be. So curvature survives; only the component that a real SDF would not have is suppressed. It is a constraint of the right shape, not a blunt smoothing.

The second reading is topological. Morse theory relates the count of critical points of a function on a region to that region's Euler characteristic. Ghost geometry is surplus critical points. Pushing det(H) → 0 annihilates critical points in pairs until only the minimum number the topology demands remains — so it removes the spurious ones and cannot remove the necessary ones.

Relaxing the Eikonal constraint

Second contribution, easy to miss. Instead of ‖∇f‖ = 1 everywhere, NSH asks only that the gradient does not vanish, and only at the input points:

Leikrelax = mean ReLU( −( ‖∇f(p)‖₂ − σmin ) ),  σmin = 0.8Eq. 12

A one-sided hinge: zero cost once the gradient is long enough. The reasoning is that for surface extraction you do not need a metrically exact distance field, you need a field whose zero crossing is well-conditioned. Relaxing frees up capacity for the other terms. Their Table 6 sweeps σmin ∈ {0.6, 0.8, 1.0} and 0.8 wins.

The full loss

The point sets differ per term and that is not incidental. det H = 0 is a statement about the thin shell; it is false in the middle of empty space, so enforcing it there fights the other terms. Getting these sets wrong is the most common way a reimplementation of this paper looks bad.

The annealing schedule is the method

τ holds at 1 for the first 20% of iterations, falls to 3·10⁻⁴ by 40%, and to 3·10⁻⁵ at the end. Early on the Hessian term dominates and the network snaps to a crude blob with the right topology; then it fades and the data terms carve in the detail. That is the “coarse-to-fine” in their abstract, and it is three lines of code. Fix τ to a constant and you get either a smooth potato or ghosts, depending on the constant — which is a good ablation to run and report.

Their settings

SIREN, 4 hidden layers × 256 units, ω₀ = 30. Adam, lr 5·10⁻⁵, 10K iterations, batch 15K query points. Qnear is one Gaussian sample per input point with σ = distance to that point's 50th nearest neighbour, so the sampling band automatically widens where the cloud is sparse.

Paper 2 · AAAI 2025DEUDF — three fixes for unsigned distance fields

Details Enhancement in Unsigned Distance Field Learning for High-fidelity 3D Surface Reconstruction. Xu, Hou, Wang, Qin, Zhang, He.

Why abandon the sign at all

An SDF cannot represent a surface that has no inside: a garment, a leaf, a sheet-metal panel, a scanned room with open walls. Force a signed field onto one and it seals the opening or inflates the sheet into a thin solid. A UDF has no such constraint — but UDFs reconstruct visibly worse than SDFs, and this paper is a careful diagnosis of why. Three causes, three fixes.

Fix 1 — stop enforcing non-negativity with the output layer

A distance must be ≥ 0, so everyone clamps the network output. Both standard ways are damaging:

  • |·| folds the function. Where the underlying field crosses zero, the fold creates a W shape — multiple local minima near the surface — so “where is the surface” becomes ambiguous, and the gradient direction flips erratically.
  • softplus is almost flat for negative inputs, so the gradient vanishes across a wide band around the surface. Training stalls exactly where you need precision, and the reconstruction comes out over-smoothed.

DEUDF's answer: use a plain unconditioned MLP — no |·|, no softplus — and let the field dip slightly negative near the surface. It is no longer a UDF in the strict sense; the surface is defined as the local minimum of |f| rather than an exact zero set. Non-negativity becomes a soft penalty instead:

Lpositive = mean exp( −100 · f(x) )Eq. 2 — no abs()

Read the sign twice

There is deliberately no absolute value. exp(−100f) explodes for negative f and is negligible for positive f — a one-sided “please stay positive”. Compare NeuralUDF's exp(−100|f|), which punishes near-zero values from both sides and therefore erases small components. The paper spends an entire Remark on this one missing pair of bars. Expect to be asked about it.

Fix 2 — use normal directions, via straddling pairs

A UDF's gradient vanishes at the surface, so you cannot constrain it there. DEUDF constrains it just off the surface instead. For each input point p with PCA normal n, sample a pair

q¹ = p + λn,   q² = p − λn,   λ ~ U(0, 0.003)§ normal alignment

and require that the field's gradient agrees with +n at q¹ and with −n at q² — which is exactly what a distance field does, since it increases away from the surface on both sides. That is the (−1)ᵏ in their Eq. 3. Flip the PCA normal and the two samples swap roles, leaving the loss unchanged: orientation-invariance by construction, so unoriented normals are enough.

Fix 3 — switch the Eikonal constraint off near the surface

‖∇f‖ = 1 holds for a UDF away from the surface, but near it the gradient genuinely shrinks and turns, so imposing it there drags the surface off the data. DEUDF weights the term by a gate that depends on the field value:

δ(d) = ( 1 + (d/ξ)⁴ )⁻¹,   Leik = mean δ(f(x)) · | ‖∇f(x)‖ − 1 |Eq. 4

Note the direction: δ is 1 at d = 0 and decays as |d| grows, so as written the weight emphasises near-surface points. The paper's stated intent is the opposite — reduce the contribution near the target surface. Plot the function yourself before you trust either reading, decide which polarity actually trains, and write down in your report which convention you used. Noticing an ambiguity like this and resolving it empirically is exactly the behaviour a supervisor wants to see. ξ anneals 0.01 → 0.002 alongside the learning rate.

Getting a mesh out of a UDF

No sign change, so no plain marching cubes. DEUDF uses DCUDF (Hou et al. 2023): march at a small positive iso-value to get a double cover — a closed shell wrapping the surface with a sheet on each side — then optimise that shell inward onto the minimal-distance surface. DCUDF is a separate paper's worth of machinery.

Scope call for three days

Implement the double cover (marching cubes at iso ≈ 0.005) and stop there. Say plainly in your report that the shrinking step is not implemented, and that DEUDF's Chamfer distance is therefore inflated for that reason alone, not because the field is wrong. Visually a double cover looks like a slightly puffy version of the right surface — recognisable, which is what matters for the reply.

Their settings

5-layer SIREN × 256, ω₀ = 60 (30 for noisy input). Weights λ₁…λ₄ = 400, 50, 40, 10 on dist / positive / normal / eikonal. Adam 5·10⁻⁵ with cosine annealing to zero. ~30 minutes per shape on a V100.

Paper 3 · ICML 2026MPF — stop asking one function to do two jobs

Metric–Phase Fields: Decoupling Distance and Sign for Thin-Structure Reconstruction from Unoriented Point Clouds. Kong, Chen, Zong, Hou, Hou, Wang, He. This is your supervisor's own paper — it is the one to get right.

The diagnosis

An SDF asks a single scalar function to simultaneously (i) report the distance to the surface and (ii) carry a globally consistent sign. On a watertight blob those two demands are compatible. On a thin sheet, an open boundary, or a non-manifold junction where three faces meet an edge, the sign is genuinely ill-defined — and forcing one function to satisfy an ill-defined constraint makes the optimisation ill-conditioned. The symptoms are specific: inflated thickness, sealed-up sheets, two nearby layers merged into one. A UDF dodges all of that by dropping the sign, and pays for it at extraction time with a cusp at the zero set and no sign change to march on.

The move

Learn two fields with one shared backbone, each responsible for one job:

r(x) = softplus( gr(x) ) ≥ 0    metric — UDF-like, distance onlyEq. 16
θ(x) ∈ ℝ    phase — carries sign where sign is meaningfulEq. 17

From the phase, a bounded soft sign indicator, with β a learnable scalar so the network chooses how sharp the inside/outside transition should be for this particular shape:

P(x) = tanh( β · θ(x) ) ∈ (−1, 1)Eq. 2

And then the composition that the paper calls a gated-metric formulation with residual phase injection:

φ(x) = r(x) · P(x) + θ(x)Eq. 3 — the one to memorise

Why that exact formula

Two short propositions justify it, and you should be able to sketch both.

Proposition 3.1 — sign consistency. Since r ≥ 0 and β > 0, P has the same sign as θ, so rP does too, so φ = rP + θ can never flip sign relative to θ. Therefore sign(φ) = sign(θ) and φ = 0 ⟺ θ = 0. The phase field alone decides where the surface is; the metric field cannot move it, only shape φ away from the interface.

Proposition 3.2 — gradient agreement. At a training point where the losses have driven r(p) = 0 and φ(p) = 0, differentiating Eq. 3 leaves ∇φ(p) = ∇θ(p). So the composite's normal at the surface is the phase field's normal — smooth and well-defined, unlike a UDF's cusp. The additive +θ residual is what keeps a live gradient path to θ even where the gated term goes inactive; without it, optimisation near the interface degenerates.

The seven loss terms, and what each one prevents

TermWhereWhat breaks without it

L_eik^rband 0 < r < δr stops being a distance. Excluded on the surface to dodge the cusp, and far away to dodge the medial axis.

A discrepancy worth raising

§5 names six weights — λalign, λeik-r, λeik-φ, λlap, λphase, λfar — and then lists five values: (0.7, 0.007, 0.0004, 0.002, 0.1). One value is missing from the published text. Bring this up. It costs him one sentence to answer and it demonstrates that you read the paper rather than skimmed it.

The payoff

Because φ is genuinely signed, you extract with plain marching cubes at level 0 — no DCUDF, no double-cover post-processing. Around a thin sheet, φ takes opposite signs on the two faces separated by a small gap, so it still crosses zero and the sheet survives. That is the sentence to lead with: MPF keeps UDF's robustness on thin structures and SDF's clean extraction, and the decoupling is what buys both.

Part 4What you actually have to build

One shared pipeline, three loss functions. Sharing the network, the samplers, the extraction and the metrics means every difference you observe is attributable to the method and not to the plumbing — which is the only way a comparison means anything.

ureca3d/
  common/diffops.py    gradient · hessian · det3x3 · laplacian     ← start here
  common/siren.py      SIREN MLP + MPF's two-head network          (done)
  common/sampling.py   Q_far · Q_near · MPF band · DEUDF pairs     (done)
  common/extract.py    marching cubes + component filtering        (done)
  common/metrics.py    Chamfer · F-score · normal consistency      (done)
  methods/nsh.py       tau schedule + 4 loss terms                 ← you
  methods/deudf.py     xi schedule + 5 loss terms                  ← you
  methods/mpf.py       compose() + 7 loss terms                    ← you
  train.py             one driver, --method {nsh,deudf,mpf}        (done)
  tests/test_math.py   property tests for everything you write     (done)

The four functions in diffops.py are the foundation — nothing runs until they do. The Hessian is the interesting one: it is the Jacobian of the gradient, so you compute the gradient with create_graph=True and then differentiate each of its three components again. Three extra backward passes per step, which is why NSH is the slowest of the three to train.

Ablations — the cheapest credibility you can buy

An ablation is the same run with one thing switched off. It proves you understand which part does the work, and each one is a single command:

  • NSH with λsingularH = 0 → ghost components should reappear. Report the component count, it is a number you can put in the table.
  • NSH with τ frozen at 1 instead of annealed → over-smoothed, detail lost.
  • DEUDF with |·| or softplus bolted onto the output → reproduces the W and the flat U from the diagram above.
  • MPF with Lalign = 0, and MPF extracting from r alone versus from φ → shows what the phase field is buying.

Part 6Traps, in the order you will hit them

  • Forgetting create_graph=True. Your gradient works, the Hessian silently returns garbage or throws, and no gradient reaches the network from any derivative-based term. The first thing to check when a loss refuses to move.
  • Evaluating a loss on the wrong point set. The single biggest quality difference between a faithful reimplementation and a poor one. det H = 0 belongs on near-surface samples only; the relaxed Eikonal belongs on input points only; MPF has four distinct sets. Re-read the table before you trust a bad result.
  • exp() overflow. exp(−100f) with f strongly negative is inf, then NaN, then a dead run. Clamp the exponent at ~20 and note that you did.
  • Reaching for ground-truth oriented normals. Easy to do by accident since the loader has them. All three methods must work from unoriented input; using the orientation is changing the problem.
  • Marching cubes at level 0 on a UDF returns nothing, and it looks like your training failed. It did not — see Part 2.
  • Comparing your Chamfer numbers to the published tables. Different shapes, different point counts, no DCUDF. Compare methods to each other on your own shapes, and say so explicitly.
  • Reporting a mean over a batch as if it were the paper's sum. Means are the sane choice — they make the weights independent of batch size — but that shifts the effective weights, so state the convention.
  • Leaving the write-up to the last hour. The most common way a good three days produces a weak deliverable.

Part 7What to send back

One page, four figures, one table. Keep it short — he will ask for detail if he wants it, and volunteering limitations is what makes the rest of the claims credible.

  2. Setup. Synthetic shapes, point counts, iterations, hardware. One short paragraph.
  3. Results. The gallery image plus the metrics table.
  4. What worked, what did not. Including the DCUDF gap and any term you could not get to converge.
  5. Two questions. The missing MPF weight, and the DEUDF attenuation polarity. Questions of that kind are evidence you read closely.
